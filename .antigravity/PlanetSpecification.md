1. You can use the following parameters to define the "base" look:Planet Type (planet_type): This is your primary "skin" selector. Categorize planets into:Gas Giants: Use banded cloud textures (like Jupiter/Saturn).Neptune-like: Use smoother, deep blue/cyan gradients.Super-Earths/Terrestrial: Use rocky, cratered, or "earth-like" textures.Mass and Radius: Use these to calculate Density ($\rho = \frac{M}{V}$).High Density: Suggests a rocky or iron-rich core (darker, metallic, or craggy textures).Low Density: Suggests a "puffy" gas giant or a water world.Equilibrium Temperature (st_teff & pl_orbsmax): * Hot ($>1000$ K): Give them a "lava world" look or glowing atmospheric tints.Cold ($<200$ K): Add ice caps, frosty textures, or white/blue hues.Habitable Zone: Use greens and liquid water blues.2. Atmospheric & Composition DataWe can't see the surface of most exoplanets, but we can detect chemicals in their air via Transmission Spectroscopy.Atmospheric Composition: Look for flags like pl_atmosphere.Water Vapor ($H_2O$): Suggests clouds and a potential blue/white marble look.Sodium ($Na$) / Potassium ($K$): Often found in "Hot Jupiters," these can absorb specific light, making the planet look extremely dark or deep "ink" blue.Methane ($CH_4$): Gives a blue/green tint (like Uranus and Neptune).Surface Composition: For rocky planets, check the Stellar Metallicity of the host star. If the star is rich in iron, the planet likely is too, which you can reflect in the soil color (reds/browns for iron oxides).
